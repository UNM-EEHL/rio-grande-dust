How this is calculated

Short-term human mortality (modeled) is computed exactly as in the mortality tool, with every scenario changing each of the four reaches at its own gauge, as in the paper, and multiplied by the value of a statistical life, the paper's Eq. (5). The central value is the one the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) currently applies, $8.71 million in 2015 inflation-adjusted terms, the mean of a Weibull distribution the US EPA fitted to 26 value of a statistical life studies (scale 9,648,168, shape 1.509588), restated as $11.82 million in 2025 inflation-adjusted terms with the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, as in the paper. The low and high choices are that distribution's 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, restated the same way. The value is applied to all modeled mortality without adjustment for age, and without discounting, since all of it is short-term. For the paper's nine scenarios at the central value, the 95% confidence intervals are the paper's own; otherwise the 95% interval comes from 2,000 joint draws of the regression coefficient and the mortality coefficients, at the chosen value of a statistical life.

The value of a statistical life is not the price of a human life. It is what people are willing to pay, added up, for small reductions in their risk of dying: if each of 100,000 people would pay $118 to cut that risk by one in 100,000, together they value one statistical life at $11.8 million.

What is not included

Only the short-term mortality of the mortality tool is valued. Illness, such as hospital admissions, emergency visits, and lost work and school days, and long-term mortality are left out, so these are likely conservative estimates of the economic damages. The value of a statistical life is restated in 2025 prices without a separate adjustment for income growth, as in the paper.
