How this is calculated

For each day of 2015 to 2024 the tool takes the population-weighted hours the wind blew from each of the four river reaches toward the Albuquerque area's US EPA air quality monitors, applies the scenario to that day's flow at the Central Avenue gauge, moves the other three reaches in the same proportion, and converts the result into a change in population-weighted PM10 with the study's headline regression coefficient. The change in PM10 is carried into human mortality with the concentration-response functions of Liu and coauthors (2019), which give 0.44% higher mortality from all-causes, 0.36% higher cardiovascular mortality, and 0.47% higher respiratory mortality for each 10 µg/m³ of PM10, applied to each month's baseline mortality in Bernalillo and Sandoval counties. The annual figure is the daily average times 365.25, the average length of a year once leap years are counted, rather than 365. The Central Avenue setting is for these public tools only and is not the study's headline: the paper applies added flow, a low-flow floor, and the dry-river comparison to each reach's own gauge, so those three scenarios give different numbers here than in the paper, while the percentage cuts and the climate scenarios are the same.

The 95% interval comes from 2,000 joint draws of the regression coefficient and the three mortality coefficients, written by the study's replication code; the tool reports the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across them. The study's own tables use a separate set of 2,000 draws, so intervals here can differ from them in the second decimal; the central estimates match exactly.

What is not included

Only short-term mortality, all ages, from PM10 is included here. Long-term exposure, hospital visits, asthma, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) are not counted, so the totals are conservative. The estimate uses same-day river flow, which the study shows understates the effect of a river bed that has been drying for weeks.
