How this is calculated

The paper's preferred regression estimates how much the log of daily PM10 at a monitor changes for each hour the wind blows toward it from a reach of the river, per unit change in the log of that reach's flow. For one place and one day, the change in PM10 is exp(β × hours × ln(new flow / flow now)) − 1, with β = −0.00231 the regression coefficient in column (2) of the paper's Table 1. For the whole area, the scenario changes the flow in each of the four reaches at its own gauge, as in the paper's Eq. (2); the change is applied on every day of 2015 to 2024 to the population-weighted downwind hours on each reach, using that reach's actual flow that day, converted into a change in PM10 with the paper's Eq. (3), and averaged across days. The climate scenarios are applied, as in the paper, to a typical year, the 2015-2024 average of each calendar day. Population weights give each monitor a weight that falls with the square of its distance from its county's 2020 population center, and the two counties are combined by their population shares, as in the paper. The paper reports no interval on the change in PM10, so the 95% interval here always comes from 2,000 joint draws of the regression coefficient.

The effect is concentrated at low flow. Because the regression uses the log of flow, PM10 responds to the percentage change in flow, so the same added water does far more when the river is low: raising a reach from 10 to 100 cfs lowers PM10 by about 12% over a full day downwind, while raising it from 90 to 100 cfs lowers it by about 0.6%.
