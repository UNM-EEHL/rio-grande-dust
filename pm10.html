How this is calculated

The study's headline regression estimates how much the log of daily PM10 at a monitor changes for each hour the wind blows toward it from a reach of the river, per unit change in the log of that reach's flow. For one place and one day, the change in PM10 is exp(b × hours × ln(new flow / flow now)) − 1, with b the regression coefficient. For the region, the scenario is set at the Central Avenue gauge and every reach moves in the same proportion that day; the change is applied on every day of 2015 to 2024 to the population-weighted downwind hours on all four reaches, using that day's actual Central Avenue flow, and averaged across days. The Central Avenue setting is for these public tools only and is not the study's headline: the paper applies added flow, a low-flow floor, and the dry-river comparison to each reach's own gauge, so those three scenarios give different numbers here than in the paper, while the percentage cuts and the climate scenarios are the same. Population weights give each monitor a weight that falls with the square of its distance from each county's population center.

The effect is concentrated at low flow. Because the regression uses the log of flow, halving the flow has the same effect whether the river is at 1,000 or 100 cfs; the study finds that the true response is steeper than that at low flow and flatter at high flow, so these numbers understate the change when the river is very low and overstate it when the river is high.
