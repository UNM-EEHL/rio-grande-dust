River Flow, Dust, and Human Health on the Middle Rio Grande

When the Rio Grande runs low through Albuquerque, more of its bed is left bare, and the people living downwind breathe more coarse particulate matter (PM10). These tools let you change how much water the river carries and see what follows for the air, for short-term mortality, and for the economic damages from that mortality.

About this project

The study follows the Middle Rio Grande from Cochiti Dam to the Isleta Diversion Dam, 64.6 miles of channel in four reaches, each with its own US Geological Survey stream gauge. Daily PM10 comes from the seven US EPA Air Quality System (AQS) monitors in the Albuquerque area. For every monitor, reach, and day, the study counts the hours the wind blew toward that monitor from that reach, within 15 degrees of the bearing from the closest point of the reach, and asks whether PM10 rises more on those hours when the reach is carrying less water. It does, and the estimate rests on hours when the wind actually blew off the river, not on how close a monitor happens to be.

The change in PM10 is then carried into short-term mortality with the concentration-response functions of Liu and coauthors (2019), using monthly baseline mortality counts for Bernalillo and Sandoval counties, and valued at the US EPA's value of a statistical life, $11.82 million in 2025 inflation-adjusted terms. All monetary estimates are in 2025 inflation-adjusted terms.

How the tools calculate the results

The results are obtained from a causally identified econometric model of the effect of Rio Grande river flows on downwind monitored PM10 that controls for weather (daily mean temperature, same-day precipitation, precipitation over the previous six days, daily mean wind speed, and hours of strong wind), for the share of fallow farmland within 3 km (1.9 miles) of each monitor and of its nearest river reach, and for seasonality and unobserved heterogeneity, through fixed effects for each monitor in each calendar month, which absorb everything that differs between monitors and across the seasons at each monitor, and fixed effects for each month of each year, which absorb everything shared across the region in a given month, such as regional drought, dust from far away, and wildfire smoke. Every number the tools use, from the regression coefficient to the daily river flows, downwind hours, PM10, and baseline mortality, comes from the study's replication code, through the website's own Stata program.

Your browser applies the study's four equations: the scenario's change in flow in each of the four reaches, the change in PM10 that follows, the change in modeled mortality, and its economic damages. Every scenario changes each reach at its own gauge. Scenarios that change the flow, add water, set a low-flow floor, or compare with a nearly dry river are applied to every day of 2015 to 2024, with the wind as it actually blew. The two climate scenarios are applied to a typical year, the 2015-2024 average of each calendar day. The 95% intervals come from 2,000 Monte Carlo draws of the regression coefficient and the mortality coefficients. A test in this website's code repository recomputes every scenario and checks that the browser matches the Stata program.

Each tool also reports the volume of water the scenario adds or removes at the Central Avenue gauge in Albuquerque, in acre-feet (AF) per year: the scenario's flow at the gauge minus the flow it actually recorded, at 1.98347 AF per cubic foot per second held for one day. That is the volume at one of the four reach gauges only; releases upstream would need to be somewhat larger, because some water is lost before it reaches Albuquerque.

What the tools do not show

The results count short-term (acute) mortality only, at all ages, from PM10, with one set of concentration-response functions (Liu and coauthors, 2019). They leave out illness, such as hospital visits and asthma, and long-term mortality, so they are likely conservative. The estimates also use same-day river flow; the study finds that mean flow over the previous 80 days more than doubles the PM10 estimate, which suggests that these results are conservative lower bounds. Scenarios far outside the flows the river carried from 2015 to 2024, or with losses of more than half the river's water, are extrapolations.
