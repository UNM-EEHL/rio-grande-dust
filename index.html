River Flow, Dust, and Human Health on the Middle Rio Grande

When the Rio Grande runs low through Albuquerque, more of its bed is left bare, and the people living downwind breathe more coarse particulate matter (PM10). These tools let you change how much water the river carries and see what follows for the air, for short-term mortality, and for the economic damages from that mortality.

About this project

The study follows the Middle Rio Grande from Cochiti Dam to the Isleta Diversion Dam, 64.6 miles of channel in four reaches, each with its own US Geological Survey stream gauge. Daily PM10 comes from the seven US EPA regulatory monitors in the Albuquerque area. For every monitor, reach, and day, the study counts the hours the wind blew from that reach toward that monitor, and asks whether PM10 rises more on those hours when the reach is carrying less water. It does, and the estimate rests on hours when the wind actually blew off the river, not on how close a monitor happens to be.

The change in PM10 is then carried into short-term mortality with the concentration-response functions of Liu and coauthors (2019), using monthly baseline mortality counts for Bernalillo and Sandoval counties, and valued at the US EPA's value of a statistical life, with all monetary estimates in 2015 inflation-adjusted terms.

How the tools calculate the results

The results are obtained from a causally identified econometric model of the effect of Rio Grande river flows on downwind monitored PM10 that accounts for any confounding effects of weather (daily temperature, same-day and recent precipitation, and wind speed), seasonality, and unobserved heterogeneity, through fixed effects for each monitor in each calendar month, which absorb everything that differs between monitors and across the seasons at each monitor, and fixed effects for each month of each year, which absorb everything shared across the region in a given month, such as regional drought, dust from far away, and wildfire smoke. Every number the tools use, from the regression coefficient to the daily river flows, downwind hours, PM10, and baseline mortality, is written by the study's replication code. Every scenario is set at the Central Avenue gauge in Albuquerque, and on each day the other three reaches move in the same proportion. That setting is for these public tools only, so you can change one familiar flow and see what follows. It is not the study's headline: the paper applies added flow, a low-flow floor, and the dry-river comparison to each reach's own gauge, so those three scenarios give different numbers here than in the paper, while the percentage cuts and the climate scenarios are the same. Your browser applies the same four equations the study does: the scenario's change in flow, the change in PM10 that follows, the change in modeled mortality, and its economic damages. The 95% intervals come from the same 2,000 joint draws of the regression coefficient and the mortality coefficients that the replication code writes. A test in this repository recomputes every scenario the replication code reports and checks that the browser matches it.

What the tools do not show

The results are for short-term (acute) mortality only, all ages, from PM10 only, with one set of concentration-response functions. Long-term mortality, hospital visits, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) are not included, so the monetary estimates are conservative. The estimate uses same-day river flow, which understates the effect of a river bed that has been drying for weeks; the study treats it as a lower bound. Scenarios far outside the flows the river actually carried from 2015 to 2024 are extrapolations.
